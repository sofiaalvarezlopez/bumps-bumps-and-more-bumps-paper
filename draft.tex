\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

As of the \acf{GWTC-5}~\citep{LIGOScientific:2026sit,LIGOScientific:2026ifv,LIGOScientific:2026wfs}, the LIGO~\citep{TheLIGOScientific:2014jea}, Virgo~\citep{TheVirgo:2014hva}, and KAGRA~\citep{KAGRA:2020tym} collaborations (\acs{LVK}) have detected hundreds of merging stellar-mass \acp{BH} via their \ac{GW} signals. Analyzing these observations jointly allows us to characterize the distributions of \ac{BBH} masses, spins, and redshifts~\citep{LIGOScientific:2026ctl}, and investigate the astrophysical processes that shape the population~\citep{Vitale:2020aaz,Thrane:2018qnx,Callister:2024cdx}.

In particular, structure in the \ac{BBH} mass distribution may contain information about the evolution of \ac{BH} progenitors and the processes by which binaries are assembled~\citep{Mandel:2018hfr, vanSon:2022ylf}. Stellar and binary evolution can preferentially produce \acp{BH} at certain mass scales~\citep{Stevenson:2019rcw,vanSon:2022myr}. The superposition of subpopulations with different formation histories~\citep[e.g.,][]{Mapelli:2020vfa,Ivanova:2012vx,Bavera:2020uch,Gerosa:2021mno,Mapelli:2019bnp,Mandel:2021smh}, may also introduce further structure in the overall mass distribution~\citep{Zevin:2020gbd,Galaudage:2026opk}. Identifying and characterizing these features can help constrain the astrophysical origins of the \ac{BBH} population.

Several features in the mass distribution have already been identified from~\ac{GW} observations, including a global peak near $10\,\Msun$ and a feature near $30\,\Msun$~\citep[e.g.,][]{LIGOScientific:2020kqk,KAGRA:2021duu,LIGOScientific:2026ctl,Edelman:2021zkw,Edelman:2022ydv,Farah:2023vsc,Rinaldi:2021bhm}. Previous studies have also reported hints of additional structure near $20\,\Msun$ and $60\,\Msun$~\citep{Tiwari:2020otp,Edelman:2022ydv,Tiwari:2021yvr,MaganaHernandez:2024qkz,Gennari:2025nho,Pierra:2026ffj}. However, the significance and morphology of these features remain uncertain, as they depend on the mass parameterizations~\citep[e.g.,][]{Tiwari:2023xff} and the population models used. For instance, while the \ac{GWTC-4} analysis reported hints of additional structure near $20\,\Msun$ and $60\,\Msun$~\citep{LIGOScientific:2025pvj}, the more recent \ac{GWTC-5} analysis found no evidence for these features in its primary-mass distribution~\citep{LIGOScientific:2026ctl}.

The identification of these features depends, in part, on the assumptions used to model the mass distribution. For instance, heuristic parametric models describe the population using simple functional forms, often motivated by astrophysical expectations (e.g., the \textsc{Power Law + Peak}~\citep{Talbot:2018cva} or the more recent \textsc{Broken Power Law + Two Peaks}~\citep{LIGOScientific:2025pvj,LIGOScientific:2026ctl} mass models). However, parametric models make strong assumptions about the population, and their ability to capture additional structure is constrained by the assumed functional forms. As a result, the inferred distributions may be biased and subject to model misspecification~\citep{Romero-Shaw:2022ctb,Cheng:2023ddt}, potentially leading to model-driven conclusions.

\red{Weakly parametric} models, on the other hand, provide greater flexibility by making fewer assumptions about the functional form of the underlying population~\citep[e.g.,][]{Heinzel:2024jlc,Edelman:2021zkw,Edelman:2022ydv,Godfrey:2023oxb,Golomb:2022bon,Tiwari:2020vym,Rinaldi:2021bhm,Callister:2023tgi,Alvarez-Lopez:2025ltt,Ray:2024hos, Ray:2026qer}. Nonparametric models are therefore able to uncover features that may not be captured by more restrictive parametric models, although their greater flexibility comes at the expense of larger statistical uncertainties. The inferred structure can still depend on modeling choices, such as the priors controlling the smoothness and flexibility of the distribution.

Mixture models provide a way to combine parametric and \red{weakly parametric} approaches by describing the population as a superposition of components with potentially different properties~\citep[e.g.,][]{Tiwari:2020vym,Godfrey:2023oxb,LIGOScientific:2025pvj,LIGOScientific:2026ctl,Rinaldi:2025evs,Alvarez-Lopez:inprep}. In particular, combining parametric and flexible components allows us to model prominent features explicitly, while allowing the flexible component to identify additional structure without prescribing its location or functional form~\citep{Godfrey:2023oxb,Alvarez-Lopez:2026ymo,Alvarez-Lopez:inprep}. %Moreover, different components can have distinct mass-ratio and spin distributions, allowing us to account for differences in the properties of the underlying subpopulations.

In this \emph{Letter}, we use two different mixture models that combine parametric components with \PixelPop~\citep{Alvarez-Lopez:inprep} and \Bsplines~\citep{Edelman:2021zkw,Edelman:2022ydv,Godfrey:2023oxb} to infer the \ac{BBH} primary-mass distribution. Despite substantially different assumptions about the parametric peaks, the flexible continuum, and the distributions of other binary parameters associated with each component, we find striking agreement between the two models. Our main findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Both mixture models recover remarkably similar primary-mass distributions, including the locations and amplitudes of additional features beyond the global peak near $10\,\Msun$. This agreement suggests that the inferred structure is robust across substantially different population models, even without making specific assumptions about the additional features \textit{a priori}.
    \item When modeling only the $10\,\Msun$ peak parametrically, both methods recover additional features near $20\,\Msun$ and $30\,\Msun$ without prescribing their functional forms. Modeling the $30\,\Msun$ feature parametrically reduces the evidence for a peak but allows greater flexibility that shows a more prominent feature near $60\,\Msun$. \sa{Maybe rephrase this a bit.}

\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

We analyze 259 events from \acs{GWTC-5} with false-alarm rates below $1\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$~\citep{LIGOScientific:2026wfs}, using the individual-event posterior samples publicly released by the \acs{LVK} collaboration~\citep{ligo_scientific_collaboration_and_virgo_2026_20348005,ligo_scientific_collaboration_and_virgo_2026_20348006}. We infer population properties using the standard hierarchical Bayesian framework for gravitational-wave population inference~\citep{Mandel:2018mve,Thrane:2018qnx,Vitale:2020aaz,Essick:2023upv}.

We model the \ac{BBH} primary-mass distribution as a mixture of parametric peaks and a flexible component (hereafter, the continuum). We describe the continuum using either \PixelPop~\citep{Heinzel:2024jlc} or \Bsplines. We refer to these models as \PPMixture~\citep{Alvarez-Lopez:inprep} and \BsplMixture~\citep{Edelman:2021zkw,Edelman:2022ydv,Godfrey:2023oxb}, respectively.

We consider two variants of each mixture model. In \oneparpeak, we model parametrically the global maximum of the $m_1$ distribution, located near $10\,\Msun$~\citep{LIGOScientific:2025pvj}. We use the continuum to infer the remaining structure, including the feature near $35\,\Msun$, without prescribing its functional form. In \twoparpeaks, we use Gaussian distributions centered around $10\,\Msun$ and $35\,\Msun$, allowing the continuum greater flexibility to resolve additional structure in $m_1$. We note that the peak prescriptions differ between \PPMixture and \BsplMixture, as detailed in App.~\ref{app:model-details}.

In both \oneparpeak and \twoparpeaks, we consider separate mass-ratio ($q$) and spin distributions for each parametric peak and for the continuum, while all components share a common redshift ($z$) evolution. In \PPMixture, we model $q$, effective spin ($\chieff$), and the redshift evolution parametrically. In \BsplMixture, we instead use \Bsplines for $q$ and the component spins, and a modulated power law for the redshift evolution. These choices allow us to compare the inferred primary-mass distribution under substantially different assumptions about the remaining population properties. We describe our modeling choices in detail in App.~\ref{app:model-details}.

Figure~\ref{fig:m1-ppd} shows the primary-mass distribution inferred with the \oneparpeak (top) and \twoparpeaks (bottom) variants. The left panels show the total distribution, $p(m_1)$, inferred with \PPMixture and \BsplMixture, along with the distribution obtained with the \textsc{Default} BBH model from \ac{GWTC-5}~\citep{LIGOScientific:2026ctl}. The right panels show the individual components of both mixture models.

In both variations, we find striking agreement between \PPMixture and \BsplMixture, despite the significantly different modeling assumptions we adopt for each model. In particular, the two methods recover similar locations and amplitudes for the additional features. With both methods, we recover the global peak near $10\,\Msun$ and additional structure at higher masses. These features are broadly consistent with the \ac{GWTC-5} \textsc{Default} \ac{BBH} model, although they are more clearly resolved by our mixture models.

With \oneparpeak, the continuum recovers two excesses near $20\,\Msun$ and $30\,\Msun$, both of which appear as local maxima in the median total distribution. We also observe a change in slope near $60,\Msun$, although no distinct local maximum is apparent at this mass. When we model the $30\,\Msun$ feature parametrically with \twoparpeaks, it no longer appears as a distinct local maximum in the median total distribution. However, the feature near $60,\Msun$ becomes more prominent, which can be most seen in the continuum of both mixture models (see bottom right panel).

We also find good agreement in the inferred mixing fractions. With \oneparpeak, we infer a continuum mixing fraction of \textcolor{red}{$\xi_c=0.36^{+0.21}_{-0.12}$} (90\% symmetric credible interval) and \textcolor{red}{$0.29^{+0.19}_{-0.13}$} for \PPMixture and \BsplMixture, respectively. With \twoparpeaks, the continuum fractions decrease to \textcolor{red}{$\xi_c=0.19^{+0.17}_{-0.09}$} and \textcolor{red}{$0.17^{+0.16}_{-0.08}$}, respectively. Both mixture models also recover consistent mixing fractions for the feature near $30\,\Msun$, with \textcolor{red}{$\xi_{30\,\Msun}=0.14^{+0.07}_{-0.05}$} and \textcolor{red}{$0.15^{+0.09}_{-0.06}$}, respectively.

To characterize the nature of the features recovered near $20$, $30$, and $60\,\Msun$ by both \PPMixture and \BsplMixture models in both \oneparpeak and \twoparpeaks variants, we examine the derivatives of $p(m_1)$ across posterior realizations. We calculate the posterior probability that these features correspond to local maxima in the primary-mass distribution and infer their locations when present. \textcolor{red}{We also investigate whether features without distinct maxima are associated with changes in slope.}

\section{Model details}\label{app:model-details}

For events in \acs{GWTC-3} and earlier catalogs, we use posterior samples generated with the \textsc{IMRPhenomXPHM} waveform model~\citep{Pratten:2020ceb}. For events in \acs{GWTC-4} and \acs{GWTC-5}, we use \textsc{NRSur7dq4} samples when available and \textsc{IMRPhenomXPHM-SpinTaylor} samples otherwise. Although our waveform choices differ slightly from those of~\citet{LIGOScientific:2026wfs}, they increase the number of posterior samples per event from \textcolor{red}{1993} to 6913, reducing the parameter-estimation contribution to the log-likelihood variance.

We account for selection effects by computing sensitivity estimates for \acs{BBH} systems over O1--O4b via a simulation campaign~\citep{Essick:2025zed,ligo_scientific_collaboration_2026_19500052}. We evaluate the population likelihood using Monte Carlo integration and limit the variance of the log-likelihood estimate to be less than one during inference~\citep{Tiwari:2017ndi,Essick:2022ojx,Talbot:2023pex,Heinzel:2025ogf}.

For \PPMixture, we model the $m_1$ continuum with \PixelPop and its mass ratio ($q$) and effective spin (\chieff) distributions parametrically. The full \PPMixture model is given by

\begin{widetext}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pp-mixture}
  p_{\rm mix}^{\rm PP}(m_1,q,\chieff) =
  \xi_c\,p_{\rm PP}(m_1)\,
  p_{\rm c}(q\,|\,m_1)\,p_{\rm c}(\chieff) +
  (1-\xi_c)\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\rm p}}
  \lambda_i\,p_{{\rm peak},i}(m_1)\,p_{i}(q\,|\,m_1)\,p_{i}(\chieff),
\end{equation}
\end{widetext}

where $\xi_c$ is the fraction of binaries in the \PixelPop component and $\lambda_i$ are the relative weights of the $N_{\rm p}$ parametric peaks, with $\sum_i \lambda_i = 1$. For \oneparpeak, $N_{\rm p}=1$ and $\lambda_1=1$; for \twoparpeaks, $N_{\rm p}=2$.

To model each peak, we use a Gaussian in $m_1$. We restrict the means of the $10\,\Msun$ and $30\,\Msun$ peaks to $[8, 12]\,\Msun$ and $[25, 40]\,\Msun$, respectively, to avoid degeneracies between the peaks. The effective spin and mass ratio distribution of each peak are also Gaussian, with $q$ truncated to $m_2 \geq 3\,\Msun$. 

To model the continuum, we use a one-dimensional \PixelPop field with 1000 bins uniform in $\ln m_1$ over $[3,300]\,\Msun$. We use a mass-independent \gtwou model for the effective spin (see~\cite{Alvarez-Lopez:2026ymo}), which consists of a mixture of a Gaussian bulk and two half-uniform components on either side of $\chieff = 0$. This model allows for structure beyond a single Gaussian, providing flexibility to describe the combined spin distribution of the $30\,\Msun$ feature alone (in \twoparpeaks) and when modeled with the rest of the continuum (in \oneparpeak). In \twoparpeaks, we use a Gaussian for the mass ratio distribution. In \oneparpeak, we use a mixture of two Gaussians, since the continuum also includes the $30\,\Msun$ feature, whose mass ratios may differ from those of the rest of the continuum. In both cases, we apply the same $m_2$ truncation as for the $q$ distributions of the peaks.

We infer the \ac{BBH} merger rate $\mathcal{R}(m_1,q,\chieff;z)
= R_0\,(1+z)^\kappa\, p_{\rm mix}^{\rm PP}(m_1,q,\chieff)$, where the redshift distribution evolves as a powerlaw with index $\kappa$~\citep{Fishbach:2018edt} and $R_0$ is the overall merger-rate scale.

The full \BsplMixture model is given by
\begin{widetext}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bspline-mixture}
\begin{aligned}
    p_\mathrm{mix}^\mathrm{BS}(z,m_1,q,a_1,a_2,\cos\theta_1,\cos\theta_2)&=p(z)\left[\xi_cp_c(m_1)p_c(q|m_1)p_c(a_1)p_c(a_2)p_c(\cos\theta_1)p_c(\cos\theta_2)\vphantom{\sum_{i=1}^{N_p}}\right. \\
    &\left.+\sum_{i=1}^{N_p}\xi_ip_{\mathrm{peak},i}(m_1)p_i(q|m_1)p_i(a_1)p_i(a_2)p_i(\cos\theta_1)p_i(\cos\theta_2)\right],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{widetext}
where \(\xi_c,\,\xi_i\) are the fractions of binaries in the continuum and peaks of the \BsplMixture, respectively.
With the exception of the shared redshift distribution and the peaks in the primary mass distribution, every distribution is modeled \red{by/with/using} \Bsplines.
The \BsplMixture models the component spins of the binary as independent and identically distributed (IID).
\red{Again}, for \oneparpeak, \(N_\mathrm{p}=1\); for \twoparpeaks, \(N_\mathrm{p}=2\).

Each peak is modeled with a log-Gaussian in \(m_1\), where the log-means \(\ln(M/M_\odot)\) of the \(10\,M_\odot\) and \(\sim30\,M_\odot\) peaks are constrained to \([\ln7,\ln15]\) and \([\ln28,\ln40]\) respectively.

The shared redshift distribution follows a modulated power-law distribution (in linear \(z\)) as in \cite{Edelman:2022ydv}:
\[
p(z)=\frac{dV_c}{dz}(1+z)^{\kappa-1}\exp\mathcal{B}(z).
\]
